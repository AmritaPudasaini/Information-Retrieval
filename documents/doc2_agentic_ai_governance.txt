Title: Agentic AI Enters Regulated Industries, Testing Governance Frameworks
Source (for reference, not reproduced): hai.stanford.edu, digitalpolicyalert.org

As artificial intelligence regulation matures, a new and harder question has emerged: what happens when an AI system does not just recommend an action but actually carries it out on its own. Agentic systems, which can complete multi step tasks without a human approving every move, are now being piloted in sectors that regulators have historically treated with extra caution, including healthcare and finance. One closely watched example is a state level pilot program that allows an autonomous AI agent to renew prescriptions for patients with chronic conditions, provided the developer follows a contract covering safety checks and data privacy. Physicians initially validate the agent's decisions, but the arrangement is expected to expand into one of the first large scale deployments of agentic AI in a clinical setting.

The move has drawn criticism from physician and pharmacist associations, who argue that care decisions should not be delegated to software regardless of how many safeguards are attached. Their concern echoes a broader debate playing out in legislatures worldwide: existing AI laws were largely written with predictive or recommendation style systems in mind, and it is not yet clear how well concepts like human oversight, explainability, and liability translate to autonomous agents that plan and execute their own sequences of actions. Financial regulators in several countries have begun drafting guidance specifically for agentic AI used in trading, lending, and customer service, recognizing that the rules built for the previous generation of AI may not be sufficient for systems that act rather than merely advise.
